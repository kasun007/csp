Why Philosophy Belongs to Everyone

September 2026 · 5 min read

Ask someone what a philosopher looks like, and they'll probably describe a man in a toga, or a professor buried in books no one else can understand. That image keeps a lot of people out of a conversation that was never meant to be exclusive.

Philosophy started as something people did together, out loud, in public. Socrates didn't lecture from behind a desk — he asked questions in the market, of anyone willing to answer. The habit of examining an idea carefully, of asking "why do we believe this?", doesn't require a degree. It requires curiosity and a willingness to be wrong.

Most people already think philosophically without naming it. A parent working out how to be fair between two children is doing ethics. A commuter wondering whether a decision was really theirs to make, or shaped entirely by circumstance, is doing philosophy of mind. The questions are already there; what's missing is often just permission to take them seriously.

At CSP, we've built courses and discussions around that idea. No prior background is assumed. What matters is that you're willing to sit with a question longer than usual, and to hear out an answer you don't already agree with.

Philosophy belongs to everyone because the questions it asks — how should I live, what do I owe others, what can I actually know — belong to everyone too. Sri Lanka has never lacked people asking these questions. What it's lacked is more rooms to ask them in, out loud, together.
