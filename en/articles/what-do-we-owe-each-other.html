What Do We Owe Each Other?

August 2026 · 6 min read

Someone cuts in line at the pharmacy. A stranger's bag falls open on a crowded bus and no one stops to help. A colleague takes credit for a shared idea. None of these moments involve breaking a law, yet something in them still feels wrong. That feeling is where ethics actually starts — not in abstract theory, but in the everyday sense that we owe each other something, even when no one is keeping score.

Philosophers have spent a long time trying to name what exactly that "something" is. Some argue it comes down to consequences: an act is right if it leads to more good than harm. Others argue duty matters more than outcome — that certain things are owed to people simply because they're people, regardless of what happens next. A third view starts from care: we owe more to those we're in relationship with, and our obligations grow out of those bonds rather than a universal rule.

None of these views fully settles the pharmacy queue or the fallen bag. But together they explain why the discomfort we feel in small moments is worth taking seriously. If we only ever ask "did I break a rule?", we miss most of what ethics is actually about — the ongoing, unglamorous work of noticing other people and adjusting our behaviour because they exist.

This is also where culture and philosophy meet. Sri Lankan traditions of hospitality, of looking out for neighbours, of extended family obligation, are already answers to the question "what do we owe each other?" — arrived at long before anyone wrote them into a philosophy textbook. Studying ethics formally doesn't replace that inheritance; it gives us a vocabulary for examining it, keeping what still serves us and questioning what doesn't.

The next time something small feels off — a queue, a bag, a shared idea gone uncredited — it's worth pausing on. That discomfort is ethics doing its job.
